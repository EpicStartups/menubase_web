The 5 POS systems worth serious consideration in Malaysia 2026

The Malaysian F&B POS market is crowded. There are at least a dozen vendors with active sales teams. Most of them are not worth serious evaluation because the support is thin, the product roadmap is unclear, or the total cost structure becomes unworkable within 18 months. The five below have the scale, the local presence and the track record to make the shortlist.

StoreHub (RM199 to RM299 per month)

Strengths. StoreHub is the most comprehensive POS system in Malaysia for F&B as of 2026. The BackOffice analytics module is genuinely powerful for operators who use it - real-time sales by item, labour cost tracking, multi-outlet stock visibility. The GrabFood and Foodpanda integration is the most developed of any local POS vendor, which matters significantly for operators doing meaningful aggregator volume. Multi-outlet rollout is relatively clean once the first outlet is configured. The customer base is large enough that you can find other operators in your area running StoreHub and ask them for the honest 90-day truth before you sign.

Weaknesses. Subscription creep is real. The base plan looks affordable but the add-ons (advanced reporting, additional staff accounts, hardware bundle, payment gateway fees, loyalty module) compound the real monthly cost well above the advertised price. Hardware is largely a locked-in bundle at the premium tier - you cannot always bring your own tablets or scanners without complications. Contract terms can be aggressive; a 24 or 36-month commitment is common in the sales conversation. Read the contract carefully before the salesperson's urgency pressure works on you.

Verdict. Strong for single-outlet casual restaurants and multi-outlet operators who need centralised management. The price is fair for what you get if you use the analytics seriously. Not the right choice if you want month-to-month flexibility.

Slurp (RM150 to RM250 per month)

Strengths. Slurp's interface is notably cleaner than StoreHub's for front-of-house staff. The onboarding experience is smoother than average. Reporting covers the core bases well - sales by item, shift summary, daily revenue. For a cafe or casual restaurant that does not need deep enterprise inventory management, Slurp covers 80 percent of the use case at a lower cost than StoreHub. The support team in Malaysia tends to respond reasonably quickly for critical issues.

Weaknesses. The ecosystem is smaller than StoreHub. Third-party integrations are fewer. Multi-outlet management at scale requires the higher tier and the cost compresses the pricing advantage quickly. The aggregator integration with GrabFood and Foodpanda is less mature than StoreHub as of 2026 - confirm the current state directly before you rely on it. The product roadmap is less publicly visible, which means you are taking more of a bet on the vendor's direction.

Verdict. A solid choice for single-outlet cafes and casual restaurants that want a clean system without the enterprise overhead. Less compelling once you cross 3 outlets.

Loyverse (free to RM89 per month)

Strengths. The free tier is the most important thing to understand about Loyverse: it is genuinely usable for small operations and not a stripped-down teaser. A single-outlet kopitiam or small cafe can run Loyverse's free plan on a tablet and have a working cloud POS for RM0 per month. The cloud-first architecture means you can see your sales from anywhere without paying for a separate analytics add-on. No contract. Month-to-month or just free. This matters enormously in a market where most operators are being pushed into 24-month commitments.

Weaknesses. Customer support is slow and primarily ticket-based rather than phone or WhatsApp. For a critical issue during dinner service, this is a real problem. Malaysia-specific features are limited compared to local vendors - the system was built for a global market, not the Malaysian regulatory environment specifically. LHDN MyInvois e-invoice integration is not natively available as of mid-2026; you need a workaround or a third-party connector. If e-invoicing compliance is important to your business right now, this is a blocking issue. See: E-Invoicing For F&B Malaysia: The LHDN Guide.

Verdict. The best value option in the market for small single-outlet operators who do not need Malaysia-specific compliance features and can tolerate slower support response times. Not the right fit for operators who need e-invoicing compliance or responsive local support.

Eats365 (RM200 to RM450 per month)

Strengths. Eats365 is built tablet-first for table service, and it shows. The customer-facing order flow, the kitchen display system (included in the package rather than as an add-on), and the table management module are among the cleanest in the market for a proper sit-down restaurant. Multi-outlet management is strong. If you run 3 or more sit-down restaurant outlets, Eats365 deserves serious evaluation. The iPad app experience is polished.

Weaknesses. The pricing sits at the high end of the market, particularly once you add hardware. iPad lock-in is a real constraint - the system is designed around Apple hardware and Android tablet support is limited as of 2026. If you want hardware flexibility or already own Android devices, this is a significant limitation. The sales process has a reputation for being aggressive and the demo can be overwhelming in ways that obscure what the real all-in monthly cost will be. Ask for the full cost projection in writing before the demo ends.

Verdict. A genuinely strong product for sit-down restaurants that can accept iPad lock-in and the premium price. Excellent kitchen display and table management. Not the right fit for operators who want hardware flexibility or a leaner monthly outlay.

BigPOS (RM150 to RM350 per month)

Strengths. BigPOS is a Malaysian company which matters for two reasons: local support with Malaysian language capability, and priority development of Malaysia-specific features. The system ships with halal-friendly operational defaults and BNM SCANeWALLET-ready payment integration, both of which matter for operators in the Malay-majority market. The pricing is competitive and the contract terms have historically been more flexible than the larger vendors.

Weaknesses. The user base is smaller than StoreHub, Slurp, or Loyverse globally, which means fewer community resources and fewer peer operators to ask for honest feedback. Third-party integrations outside the Malaysian F&B ecosystem are limited. Regional support quality varies depending on where your outlet sits - operators in Klang Valley tend to report better support than operators in East Malaysia or smaller states. The product roadmap is less transparent than the larger competitors.

Verdict. Worth serious consideration for halal-certified or halal-conscious operators who want a Malaysian company with local compliance priority. The smaller footprint is a real trade-off but the local alignment compensates for it in many cases.

What to actually compare (not the feature list)

Every POS vendor demos well. Every feature checklist looks comprehensive. The things that actually determine whether you made the right choice show up 90 days after go-live, not in the sales presentation. Here are the 8 questions that cut through the noise.

1. Contract length and exit terms

The first question to ask any vendor is not "what can your system do" but "what are my exit terms if I need to leave in 12 months". The Malaysian F&B tech market moves fast. A contract signed in 2024 can lock you into a system that has fallen behind by 2026. Ask for the contract in writing before any commitment. A vendor who resists showing you the exit terms upfront is telling you something important about what those terms look like. See: The Hidden Costs In Your POS Subscription.

2. Real total monthly cost after add-ons

The advertised monthly price is almost never the real monthly price. Get a quote that includes: base subscription, payment processing fees (typically 0.5 to 1.5 percent per transaction on top of the base), cloud storage fees, reporting module fees, per-station or per-device fees, customer support tier fees, and the cost of the hardware bundle. Stack all of it. The real number is often 30 to 60 percent above the headline price. Build the spreadsheet before you sign.

3. Multi-outlet rollout cost

If you plan to grow to 3 or more outlets, get the per-outlet pricing now. Some vendors price multi-outlet competitively at the first outlet and hit you with steep per-outlet fees at scale. The math changes significantly at outlets 3, 5 and 10. Model it to outlet 6 before you commit to a vendor at outlet 1.

4. Hardware compatibility

Can you bring your own tablets, receipt printers, and barcode scanners? Or are you locked into the vendor's hardware bundle? Vendor hardware bundles are often marked up 30 to 50 percent above retail and are a meaningful one-time cost. More importantly, when the vendor stops selling a specific hardware model, you will be pushed to upgrade on the vendor's timeline, not yours. Hardware flexibility is a real financial consideration over a 3-year horizon.

5. LHDN MyInvois integration status

Malaysia's e-invoicing mandate from LHDN is live for larger businesses and rolling out progressively to SMEs. If your annual turnover crosses the applicable threshold, you need a POS that can generate LHDN-compliant e-invoices. Ask the vendor: "What is your MyInvois integration status today, not on the roadmap?" A roadmap promise is not the same as a live integration. An operator who signs and then discovers the integration is 6 months away has a compliance problem. Full guide: E-Invoicing For F&B Malaysia: The LHDN Guide.

6. Aggregator integration reality

Many POS vendors claim GrabFood and Foodpanda integration. The reality of "integration" varies significantly. Some vendors genuinely sync the order directly into the POS. Others route the order through the aggregator's own merchant dashboard and the "integration" is a reporting sync only. Ask to see a live demo of an aggregator order flowing from customer tap-to-order through to your POS kitchen printer. If the vendor cannot demo it live, assume the integration is incomplete.

7. Customer support response time and language

Support during a critical issue at 7:30pm on a Friday night is the real test of a vendor relationship. Ask the vendor: "What is your typical response time for a P1 issue during dinner service?" Ask for it in writing in the contract. Ask other operators what their experience has been. Vendors who promise "24/7 support" often mean a ticketing system where 24 hours is the response target, not 4 minutes. In Malaysia specifically, support in Bahasa Malaysia or Mandarin can matter for your team's ability to resolve issues on shift without involving the owner.

8. Data export

If you leave, can you take your data? All of it? In a usable format (CSV, not a proprietary export that only their next product can read)? The data in your POS - every transaction, every item sold, every customer record - is yours. A vendor who makes data export difficult or expensive is building a switching cost into your relationship. Confirm data portability in writing before signing.

Below is an honest summary of where each system sits on the 8 criteria above. This is based on public information, operator feedback and our own observations as of mid-2026. These things change. Always verify current status with the vendor directly before signing.

StoreHub: 12 to 36 months common; negotiate hard for shorter initial term. Slurp: 12 to 24 months; some month-to-month available at higher price tier. Hardware flexibility

StoreHub: Preferred hardware list; BYOD possible with limitations. Slurp: Relatively flexible; works with standard Android tablets and common receipt printers. Loyverse: Highly flexible; works on consumer tablets, no proprietary hardware required. Eats365: iPad-native; Android support limited; bring-your-own is effectively not a viable path. BigPOS: Flexible; works with standard hardware in most configurations.

StoreHub: Integration live or near-live as of mid-2026; confirm current status. Slurp: In progress as of mid-2026; confirm current status before signing if compliance is immediate. Customer support

Data portability

StoreHub: CSV export available; confirm completeness of export before signing. Slurp: Export available; verify format. Loyverse: Good data portability; exports are clean and standard. Eats365: Export available; confirm format and completeness. BigPOS: Export available; confirm format.

Which POS for which venue type

The right POS depends on your venue type, your stage of growth, and how much complexity you actually need. Here is the honest match-up.

Single-outlet kopitiam

Loyverse free tier, full stop. A kopitiam does not need enterprise inventory management, multi-outlet centralisation or kitchen display systems. The free tier handles order-taking, basic reporting and cloud sync. The RM150 or more per month you save versus a paid POS is real money in a margin-thin business. Add a basic receipt printer (RM300 to RM600 one-off) and you are running a cloud POS for near-zero monthly cost. The trade-off is slower support and no native e-invoicing, both of which are manageable at kopitiam scale in most cases.

Single-outlet cafe

Slurp or BigPOS. Both have the clean interface that fits a cafe's order flow. Both are priced appropriately for a single-outlet operation. Slurp edges ahead on UI polish. BigPOS edges ahead for halal-conscious operators or those who want local company alignment. Both beat StoreHub's pricing at single-outlet scale for a cafe that does not need enterprise features.

Single-outlet casual restaurant

StoreHub or Eats365. Table service complexity, kitchen display needs and the value of proper analytics start to justify the higher price point at casual restaurant scale. Eats365 wins on kitchen display and table management out of the box. StoreHub wins on ecosystem breadth and aggregator integration. If your team runs iPads and you want the cleanest table service experience, Eats365. If you want broader integrations and proven multi-outlet optionality, StoreHub.

Single-outlet bubble tea shop

StoreHub or BigPOS. Bubble tea ordering has specific requirements: fast throughput, complex modifiers (ice level, sugar level, toppings), and often a meaningful aggregator component. StoreHub handles modifiers well and has the best aggregator integration. BigPOS handles modifiers cleanly and is the right choice for halal-certified bubble tea operators who want local compliance priority.

Multi-outlet (3 to 5 sites)

StoreHub or Eats365. At this scale, centralised management, cross-outlet reporting and consistent hardware become the critical needs. StoreHub's BackOffice is the strongest centralised management tool in the market for this scale. Eats365 is the right answer if your concept is primarily sit-down table service and the iPad ecosystem is acceptable. Neither is cheap at this scale - budget for the real all-in cost before you commit.

Multi-outlet (6 or more sites)

Enterprise negotiation with any of the five. At 6 or more outlets you have meaningful commercial leverage. None of the five vendors will lose a 6-outlet deal on price alone. Get competitive quotes from at least 3, negotiate on contract length rather than monthly price, and insist on a 90-day performance clause. At this scale, the quality of your dedicated account manager matters more than the headline feature list. Ask who your account manager will be. Meet them before you sign.

Common operator POS mistakes

These are the decisions operators regret most, observed across the outlets we have worked with. They are not unique to any single vendor - they are structural patterns that appear regardless of which system you choose.

Signing a 36-month contract for the discount

The vendor offers a 15 percent discount for a 3-year commitment. It feels financially rational. The problem is that the Malaysian F&B tech tooling market moves on a 12 to 18 month innovation cycle. A commitment signed in early 2024 can lock you into a platform that has fallen two generations behind by late 2026. The discount rarely compensates for the loss of flexibility. Negotiate for the same discount on a 12-month rolling term. If the vendor will not budge, pay the extra 15 percent and keep the exit option. Related: The Real Pain Points Of Malaysian SME F&B Operators.

Choosing based on the demo

Every POS vendor demos well. The system is configured specifically for the demo. The sales rep has run the demo 400 times. The edge cases, the support response at 8pm, the billing dispute after month 4, the feature that is "on the roadmap" still - none of these appear in a demo. The correct approach is to get the names of 3 current operators in a similar venue type from the vendor, contact them independently, and ask: "What would you change if you were starting over?" That 15-minute conversation is worth more than any demo.

Letting the POS vendor's payment processing become your only option

Many POS vendors offer integrated payment processing at attractive rates that gradually lock you in. Once your entire payment flow runs through the vendor's gateway, switching becomes operationally disruptive. Always keep at least one independent merchant agreement active. The redundancy matters during outages. The negotiating leverage matters at renewal time. A vendor who knows you cannot easily switch payment processors has more pricing power over you at every contract renewal.

Buying every add-on in the first month

The vendor's upsell sequence is: sign the base, then add the loyalty module, then the advanced reporting, then the staff management module, then the marketing tools. Each one looks reasonable individually. Together, they double the base price. The discipline is to run with the base package for 60 days, identify which specific operational problem is not solved, and only then add the specific module that solves it. Audit your add-ons at month 6 and kill anything you are not using actively.

Underestimating staff training time

Malaysian F&B floor staff turnover averages 6 to 9 months. Even if the initial training goes well, you will retrain a large fraction of your team within the first year. Budget 2 to 3 weeks for the team to genuinely settle into a new POS system, not the 2-day estimate the vendor quotes. Schedule the go-live for a quiet period, not the week before Hari Raya. Plan for one experienced operator on each shift for the first two weeks who can handle the inevitable edge cases without calling support.

The questions to ask before signing

Take these into the final sales meeting with any POS vendor. Write the answers down. If the vendor cannot answer them clearly and in writing, that is important information.

"What is the all-in monthly cost including every add-on you will suggest in the first 6 months?" Get a number, not a range. If the vendor says they cannot estimate, ask them to itemise every add-on they have ever sold to a similar-sized operator and price each one.

"Can I cancel on 30 days notice?" If no, what is the minimum notice period and what are the financial penalties for early exit? Read the exact contract language.

"Can I export all my data in CSV format at any time?" Confirm it includes transaction history, item-level data and any customer records captured. Confirm there is no fee for the export.

"What is your MyInvois integration status today, not on the roadmap?" If integration is live, ask for a demo of an e-invoice being generated in the system. If it is on the roadmap, get a written commitment date and ask what happens to your subscription if it misses that date.

"What is your typical response time for a P1 issue during dinner service on a Friday night?" Get a specific number in writing. Then ask the 3 current operators you spoke to independently whether that number matches their experience.

"Who will be my dedicated account manager after the first 30 days?" The sales rep who closes you will likely not be the support contact after signing. Meet the account manager before you commit. Their responsiveness and knowledge during that meeting predicts how the relationship will feel at month 18.
